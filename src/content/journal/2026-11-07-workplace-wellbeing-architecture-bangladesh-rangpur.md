A person can spend eight or nine hours at a desk without giving much thought to the building around them. They adjust the chair, lower the screen brightness, close the blinds or turn up the air conditioning. These small adjustments become part of the working day.

But what happens when the discomfort is built into the space itself? A desk may be too far from a window. The room may have little fresh air. Sunlight may fall directly across a computer screen. There may be nowhere quiet to concentrate or somewhere comfortable to take a short break.

As an architect, I have become interested in how these everyday conditions affect the way people experience a workplace—and what **workplace wellbeing architecture in Bangladesh** looks like when it starts with the building user. The question became especially important during my Bachelor of Architecture thesis at Hajee Mohammad Danesh Science and Technology University. My [research focused on a proposed human-centred work environment for Rangpur Hi-Tech Park](/research).

I wanted to understand what employees actually noticed about their offices before deciding what the building should provide. I began with a small survey of IT and IT-enabled services professionals. Their answers helped me look at workplace design from a different point of view: the view of the person who has to use the building every day.

## Why employee wellbeing belongs in the architectural brief

Workplace design discussions often begin with floor area, construction cost, rental space and the number of people a building can accommodate. These are necessary decisions, particularly for technology offices and shared commercial buildings. Yet they do not tell us whether the resulting space will be comfortable to work in.

A workplace also needs to support concentration, movement, communication and rest. People spend long periods looking at screens. They need suitable lighting, comfortable temperatures, manageable noise levels and a degree of control over their immediate surroundings. Access to daylight, fresh air and views of greenery can also change how an office feels.

In Bangladesh, these questions are shaped by a warm and humid climate, strong seasonal rain and differences between the hot months and the cooler winter period. As I explored in [Why Office Buildings in Bangladesh Need More Than Air Conditioning](/blogs/climate-responsive-office-design-bangladesh), a building that relies on sealed interiors and air conditioning for every hour of the year may miss opportunities to use shade, natural light and outdoor spaces more thoughtfully.

This is why workplace wellbeing architecture in Bangladesh needs to consider the daily experience of employees. I use the term **human-centred workplace design** for an approach that makes those needs part of the design brief, rather than treating the workstation count as the only measure of a successful office.

## What I wanted to learn from IT professionals

The study was part of my undergraduate thesis, [*Designing a Human-Centered Work Environment for the Employees of Rangpur Hi-Tech Park*](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23915357). I completed the original research in 2021. The 2026 revised edition updates the industry context and rechecks the calculations, while retaining the original survey data.

The survey was conducted remotely during the COVID-19 lockdown in 2020. I shared a short questionnaire through WhatsApp and received 30 complete responses from IT and IT-enabled services employees across nine companies. The participants worked in different roles, including software development, systems analysis, quality assurance, project management, UX design and web development.

I asked respondents to rate six aspects of their working environment on a scale from 1 to 5. These included physical comfort, ergonomic comfort, light and ventilation, concentration, mental comfort and perceived productivity.

They first rated their existing workplace. They were then given a description of a proposed human-centred workplace, with features such as ergonomic seating, daylight without glare, ventilation and temperature control, views of greenery, breakout spaces and quiet focus areas. They rated how they expected that environment to perform.

That distinction is important. The second set of ratings described an environment the respondents imagined. They did not come from employees working inside a completed building.

## What the survey told me

The lowest average rating for existing workplaces was for light, ventilation and greenery, at **2.93 out of 5**. Physical comfort followed at **3.00**. Ergonomic comfort and mental comfort received slightly higher average ratings, at **3.20** and **3.23**.

When respondents considered the proposed workplace described in the questionnaire, they expected higher ratings across all six areas. The largest expected change was again in light, ventilation and greenery.

| Workplace dimension | Existing workplace average | Expected rating for described workplace |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Light, ventilation and greenery** | **2.93** | **4.07** |
| Concentration | 3.13 | 4.17 |
| Physical comfort | 3.00 | 3.97 |
| Mental comfort | 3.23 | 4.13 |
| Ergonomic comfort | 3.20 | 4.07 |
| Perceived productivity | 3.20 | 3.90 |

These figures describe respondents' ratings and expectations (which you can explore in detail on the [interactive research dashboard](/research)). They do not show that a new workplace improved comfort or productivity in practice. Still, the pattern helped me identify what people felt was missing from their current environments.

The survey also asked about common experiences during a working week. In this small sample, **86.7%** reported back or neck discomfort sometimes or more often. Eye strain after screen work was reported by **80%**, while **76.7%** reported work-related stress or mental fatigue sometimes or more often.

These were self-reported symptoms, not medical diagnoses. The survey cannot establish that office design caused them. It does, however, make a useful case for taking the physical working environment seriously.

One question asked participants to choose the single most important improvement they would make if they could select only one. Better ventilation and temperature control received eight votes, or **26.7%**. Views of greenery from the workstation received the same number. Ergonomic furniture came next, with five votes.

I found this particularly interesting. Furniture can often be changed after a tenant moves into an office. The location of a window, the depth of a floor plate, the direction of the sun and the possibility of creating a courtyard are much harder to change later. Those are architectural decisions, and they need to be considered early.

## Turning employee responses into design decisions

The survey did not provide a ready-made building design. It helped define questions that I could then explore through site analysis, literature and architectural planning.

The [proposed Rangpur Hi-Tech Park site](/work) covers about 8.59 acres at Khalishakuri, Topodhan. During site visits, I observed the existing vegetation, including fruit trees, and looked at the site's relationship with nearby roads, drainage and surrounding development.

The design proposal organised the programme into four blocks around a central courtyard. This arrangement was intended to bring more work areas close to windows and views, provide a shared outdoor space and avoid placing the entire programme in one deep building mass. The courtyard became part of the workplace circulation and social life, rather than leftover space between buildings.

Several design decisions grew from the research (which I also examine from an environmental perspective in [Designing Better Workplaces for Bangladesh's Climate](/blogs/passive-design-strategies-bangladesh)):

### Bringing daylight and views closer to workstations

Deep floor plans can leave the middle of an office far from windows. A more compact plan, or a group of narrower building wings, can give more people access to daylight and an outdoor view. Window placement and shading still need to be designed carefully to avoid glare and unwanted heat.

### Making room for air movement

The arrangement of rooms, corridors and openings affects whether air can move through a building. Cross-ventilation may be useful when outdoor conditions are suitable. During very hot or humid periods, or when outside air is polluted or noisy, mechanical systems may still be needed. For this reason, I considered a mixed-mode approach rather than assuming that natural ventilation would work throughout the year.

### Providing places for different kinds of work

A workplace needs spaces for collaboration, private conversations and focused tasks. These uses can conflict when they are all placed in one open room. In the Rangpur proposal, shared and collaborative areas were considered alongside quieter focus spaces. The aim was to support communication without making concentration difficult.

### Treating the courtyard as useful space

The central courtyard was planned as a place for daylight, views, informal meetings and short breaks. Planting, shade and seating were included so that employees could use it during the working day. Its value depends on whether it is comfortable and accessible, not simply on whether it appears in the masterplan.

### Keeping landscape in the conversation

Existing trees were part of the site character. The proposal explored how vegetation could be retained and connected to outdoor spaces. Trees take time to grow, so recording existing landscape before fixing the building footprint is an important early step.

## A workplace needs more than a good floor plan

The research also made me think about what usually gets protected when a project faces budget pressure. Rentable office area is easy to count. A shaded courtyard, a shared gathering space or a generous circulation area may appear less essential when a project is reduced to a cost-per-square-foot calculation.

Yet these spaces can affect how employees meet, take breaks and move through the building. They are part of the workplace experience. If they are removed at the end of the design process, the building may lose some of the qualities that made the original proposal human-centred.

This does not mean every office needs the same courtyard, layout or amount of shared space. The right answer depends on the site, climate, work culture, budget and operating model. It does mean that these needs should be discussed with clients and users before the plan is fixed.

## What this study cannot tell us

I think it is important to be clear about the limits of the research. A small study can provide useful questions and design directions, but it cannot answer everything.

First, the survey included only 30 respondents. They were recruited through convenience and snowball sampling, so they do not represent the full ICT workforce in Bangladesh. The sample also did not include anyone over the age of 45.

Second, the survey took place remotely during the 2020 COVID-19 lockdown. The questionnaire asked participants to think about one workplace, but it did not record clearly whether each person was rating their usual corporate office or their home working environment. The questionnaire was also not piloted with a separate test group before distribution.

Third, the proposed workplace ratings were based on a description. They reflect what participants expected from a better environment. They cannot be treated as evidence that the design would increase actual productivity by 21.9%, or that employees would experience the same improvements after moving into the building.

Finally, the Rangpur Hi-Tech Park scheme is an academic design proposal. It has not been built or tested through an occupied-building study. The drawings were reviewed against the design attributes developed in the thesis, but that is different from measuring daylight, temperature, air movement, noise or employee experience in a completed building.

These limitations do not make the research useless. They show what the next stage should look like.

## Why buildings need to be evaluated after people move in

Architects make predictions when they design a building. We estimate how daylight will enter, where air might move, how people will use shared spaces and whether the rooms will support different activities. Some of those predictions can be tested through simulation. Others need to be checked after the building is occupied.

A post-occupancy evaluation does exactly that. It looks at how a building performs in everyday use. For a workplace in Bangladesh, an evaluation could take place after six and twelve months, including visits during both the monsoon and the cooler season.

The process could measure light levels at desks, indoor temperature and humidity, air movement, carbon dioxide and noise. It could also record how often employees use the courtyard and other shared spaces. Repeating the same employee questionnaire would help compare what people expected with what they actually experience.

This kind of follow-up would help architects, building owners and authorities learn from completed projects. It would also make future design decisions more grounded in evidence from local workplaces.

## A more human way to think about office design

My Rangpur Hi-Tech Park thesis began with a simple question: what does an employee need from the building where they spend much of the day?

The survey brought attention to ventilation, temperature and views of greenery. The design process then explored how building form, courtyard space, daylight, landscape and different work settings could respond to those concerns.

The study is limited, and the proposed design still needs to be tested in real use. But it has shaped the way I approach workplace architecture. I now see the employee's daily experience as something that should influence the brief from the beginning, alongside area schedules, structure and cost.

A healthy workplace is not created by one feature. It comes from many decisions made together, from the position of a window to the way a shared outdoor space is planned. Those decisions are easier to make when the people who will use the building are part of the conversation before construction begins.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is human-centred workplace design?

Human-centred workplace design considers how people use and experience an office. It looks at comfort, concentration, daylight, ventilation, ergonomics, privacy, social interaction and access to nature when shaping the building and its spaces.

### How can office design support employee wellbeing in Bangladesh?

Designers can respond to the local climate through suitable orientation, external shading, useful daylight, ventilation when outdoor conditions allow, shaded outdoor areas and access to greenery. Quiet spaces, ergonomic planning and shared areas also contribute to the everyday experience of work.

### What did the Rangpur Hi-Tech Park survey find?

In a survey of 30 IT and IT-enabled services professionals, light, ventilation and greenery received the lowest average rating for existing workplaces, at 2.93 out of 5. Better ventilation and temperature control, and views of greenery from workstations, were joint top choices when respondents selected one priority for improvement.

### Does the study prove that human-centred design increases productivity?

No. Respondents gave higher expected ratings to a described human-centred workplace, including for perceived productivity. They did not work in a completed version of the proposed design. The study therefore measures expectations, not actual productivity gains.

### What is a post-occupancy evaluation?

A post-occupancy evaluation is a review of a building after people begin using it. It can combine environmental measurements, observation and feedback from occupants to see whether the building performs as intended.
