In Bangladesh, discussions about office comfort often begin with air conditioning. Developers consider cooling capacity, facility managers adjust temperature settings, and building owners focus on electricity consumption. However, I believe the conversation around climate-responsive office design in Bangladesh needs to begin much earlier, at the architectural design stage.

How much heat enters the building? Does the facade respond to the sun? Can natural light reach the workstations without creating glare? Is there an adequate supply of fresh air? And perhaps most importantly, how do these environmental conditions influence the people who spend most of their working day inside?

These questions are particularly relevant in a country such as Bangladesh, where high temperatures, humidity, seasonal rainfall and rapid urbanisation create complex environmental challenges for buildings.

Air conditioning is necessary in many commercial spaces. But depending on it as the primary solution to indoor comfort can create unnecessary energy demand while leaving other aspects of the indoor environment unresolved.

The challenge is not simply to cool office buildings. It is to design workplaces that respond to the local climate while supporting the comfort and wellbeing of their occupants.

## 1. Understanding the Urban Heat Problem in Bangladesh

The relationship between buildings and their surrounding environment is becoming increasingly important in Bangladesh's rapidly growing cities.

Urban development replaces natural surfaces with buildings, roads and other infrastructure. These changes influence how heat is absorbed, stored and released within urban areas. The reduction of vegetation and changes in urban geometry can further modify local microclimates.

This phenomenon is commonly known as the Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect.

A study by Tabassum et al. (2024), examining meteorological records from 1995 to 2019, investigated the relationship between urban and rural temperatures in Dhaka. The researchers reported an average urban heat island intensity of 0.48°C and an average daily maximum intensity of 2.15°C. They also identified an increasing trend of 0.21°C per decade.

These findings highlight the changing thermal conditions of Dhaka and the importance of understanding urban climate when planning new developments.

Air-conditioning systems also release rejected heat through their outdoor units. In densely built areas, this can add to local heat exposure, although it is only one component of the wider urban heat island process.

For architects and urban planners, this raises an important question: can buildings be designed to reduce their contribution to urban heat rather than simply responding to it?

Vegetation, shaded pedestrian spaces, appropriate building materials, building orientation and urban open spaces should all become part of the discussion.

## 2. Why Building Facade Design Matters More Than We Think

One of the most visible changes in contemporary commercial architecture is the increasing use of extensive glazed facades.

Glass provides transparency and natural views. It can also create a strong architectural identity. However, the environmental performance of a glazed building depends on its orientation, glazing properties, shading and the amount of exposed window area.

In Bangladesh's hot and humid climate, poorly designed glass facades can allow substantial solar heat gain, particularly on east- and west-facing elevations.

This creates additional cooling demand and can produce uncomfortable conditions near windows, even when the rest of the office is mechanically cooled.

The answer is not to eliminate glass from contemporary architecture. Instead, glazing should be used according to the environmental requirements of the building.

A particularly relevant study by Rana et al. (2022), *Impact Assessment of Window to Wall Ratio on Energy Consumption of an Office Building of Subtropical Monsoon Climatic Country Bangladesh*, investigated different facade configurations through energy simulation.

The researchers examined approximately 216 design alternatives across eight major cities in Bangladesh. Their findings suggested that a window-to-wall ratio (WWR) of 30% to 40% was an effective range for the air-conditioned office-building prototype studied. An existing office building in their analysis achieved an estimated 9.4% reduction in electricity consumption through an optimised window-to-wall ratio.

This finding is useful because it demonstrates that relatively fundamental architectural decisions can influence building energy performance.

However, the suggested ratio should not be treated as a universal rule. Every building requires consideration of its orientation, surrounding structures, daylight requirements, shading arrangements and material specifications.

For me, the larger lesson is that facade design should not be separated from environmental performance.

## 3. Thermal Comfort Is More Than a Temperature Setting

Another issue in conventional office design is the assumption that a particular indoor temperature will provide comfort for everyone.

In practice, thermal comfort is influenced by several interacting factors, including air temperature, relative humidity, air movement, surrounding surface temperatures, clothing and physical activity.

This explains why two people working in the same air-conditioned office can experience completely different thermal conditions.

In some offices, occupants may feel uncomfortably cold while others continue to experience discomfort from heat and humidity. Simply reducing the thermostat setting does not necessarily resolve the problem.

Thermal comfort should therefore be considered as part of a wider indoor environmental strategy.

Climate-responsive design can contribute by reducing direct solar exposure, improving shading, controlling unwanted heat gain and supporting appropriate air movement.

Depending on the building's operating conditions, naturally ventilated, mechanically ventilated or mixed-mode strategies may be appropriate.

The objective should be to provide comfortable conditions without creating an unnecessary dependence on mechanical cooling.

## 4. Indoor Air Quality: Cooling Is Not the Same as Ventilation

Temperature is only one component of a healthy indoor environment. Indoor air quality in office buildings is equally important, particularly in commercial buildings where many people occupy enclosed spaces for extended periods.

A conventional split air-conditioning unit primarily cools and recirculates indoor air. It does not, by itself, provide an adequate supply of outdoor air.

As a result, an office can feel cold while still having inadequate ventilation.

Without an appropriate ventilation strategy, indoor pollutants and occupant-generated carbon dioxide can accumulate. Materials such as paints, adhesives, furniture and cleaning products can also contribute to indoor pollutant levels.

This is why architects and building-services engineers need to consider ventilation during the early design stages.

For larger commercial developments, a Dedicated Outdoor Air System (DOAS) is one possible approach. It conditions outdoor air separately before delivering it to occupied spaces. Depending on its configuration, the system can incorporate filtration, dehumidification and energy recovery.

These systems require careful engineering. Bangladesh's high outdoor humidity makes moisture removal particularly important, and the overall performance depends on equipment selection, controls, maintenance and the building's occupancy.

International research also provides useful evidence of the relationship between indoor environmental quality and human performance.

Allen et al. (2016), in a controlled exposure study published in *Environmental Health Perspectives*, examined 24 participants over six working days. The researchers reported higher cognitive-function test scores under Green and Green+ indoor environmental conditions compared with conventional conditions.

The reported differences were 61% and 101%, respectively. These were cognitive-function test results from a controlled experiment in the United States, not measured productivity increases in Bangladeshi workplaces.

Nevertheless, the study demonstrates why ventilation and indoor environmental quality deserve greater consideration in workplace design.

## 5. What My Research in Rangpur Revealed About Workplace Design

My interest in human-centred and biophilic workplace design developed during my undergraduate research at Hajee Mohammad Danesh Science and Technology University.

My thesis, [*Designing a Human-Centered Work Environment for the Employee of Rangpur Hi-Tech Park*](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23915357) ([read the full thesis PDF on Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/23915357/files/Designing%20a%20Human-Centered%20Work%20Environment%20for%20the%20Employee%20of%20Rangpur%20Hi-Tech%20Park_Amitabh%20Chondro%20Roy.pdf?download=1) or explore the [interactive survey dashboard](/research)), investigated how the physical environment of an office relates to employees' comfort, concentration and workplace experience.

The research included a questionnaire survey of 30 IT and IT-enabled services employees working in conventional offices in Bangladesh. The survey was conducted in 2020 during the COVID-19 restrictions.

Respondents evaluated six dimensions of their existing workplaces and then rated their expectations for a described human-centred workplace.

One of the most interesting findings concerned light, ventilation and greenery.

This dimension received the lowest average rating among the six dimensions examined, at **2.93 out of 5**.

| Workplace dimension | Existing workplace rating (out of 5) |
| :--- | :--- |
| Physical comfort | 3.00 |
| Ergonomic comfort | 3.20 |
| **Light, ventilation and greenery** | **2.93** |
| Concentration | 3.13 |
| Mental comfort | 3.23 |
| Perceived productivity | 3.20 |

The findings also revealed an interesting difference in employees' priorities.

When respondents were asked to identify the single most important workplace improvement, eight of the 30 respondents selected better ventilation and temperature control. Another eight of the 30 selected views of greenery from their workstations.

Each represented **26.7%** of the sample.

Together, these two preferences accounted for **53.3%** of the respondents.

I found this particularly interesting because both improvements are closely related to architectural planning and environmental design.

Ventilation depends on how buildings are organised and how their openings and mechanical systems are configured. Visual connections with greenery depend on the relationship between workstations, windows, courtyards and outdoor spaces.

These findings should be interpreted within their limitations. The survey used a small convenience and snowball sample (n = 30). It does not represent the entire IT workforce of Bangladesh. The improved workplace was hypothetical, so its ratings reflect expectations rather than actual post-occupancy experience.

Nevertheless, the study helped me understand why workplace design should consider more than the provision of desks, equipment and cooling systems.

### Translating Research into Architectural Design

The findings informed my [architectural design proposal for the Rangpur Hi-Tech Park](/work) site at Khalishakuri.

Rather than concentrating the programme in a single large building, I organised the proposed development into four blocks around a central shaded courtyard.

The design explored several environmental strategies:

- A courtyard-centred spatial arrangement.
- Daylight access and reduced glare.
- Cross-ventilation opportunities.
- Green views from the working environment.
- Shaded pedestrian connections and semi-outdoor spaces.
- Shared areas for social interaction and relaxation.

The intention was to make environmental quality part of the architectural organisation rather than treating greenery and outdoor spaces as additional decorative features.

This was an independent academic design proposal, not the official design of the Bangladesh Hi-Tech Park Authority. Its environmental performance has not been verified through measurements of an occupied building.

For me, the most important outcome of this research was understanding how information gathered from building users can influence architectural decisions.

## 6. Learning from Traditional Architecture and Courtyard Planning

Climate-responsive architecture does not always require complicated technological solutions.

Many traditional architectural approaches in South Asia developed in response to local environmental conditions. Courtyards, shaded verandas, deep overhangs and transitional spaces have historically helped people manage sunlight, heat and everyday outdoor activities.

These passive cooling strategies remain relevant to contemporary architecture in Bangladesh.

A well-designed courtyard can provide access to daylight, visual openness and opportunities for ventilation. Its effectiveness, however, depends on its dimensions, orientation, shading, surrounding building heights and local wind conditions.

Similarly, shaded terraces and semi-outdoor circulation spaces can improve the relationship between indoor and outdoor environments.

This does not mean traditional solutions can simply be copied into contemporary office buildings. They need to be reconsidered according to modern building functions, occupancy patterns and technical requirements.

I believe the real opportunity lies in combining traditional spatial intelligence with contemporary environmental analysis.

## 7. Practical Design Strategies for More Climate-Responsive Offices

Moving towards energy-efficient office buildings in Bangladesh requires coordination between architecture, landscape design and building services.

Some strategies deserve particular attention during the early stages of commercial building design.

### Climate-responsive building orientation

The positioning of a building should respond to solar exposure, prevailing winds, surrounding structures and site conditions. Early climate analysis can help identify opportunities to reduce unnecessary heat gain.

### External solar shading

Horizontal overhangs, vertical fins, louvers and recessed openings can reduce direct solar exposure. Their design should respond to the orientation of individual facades rather than following a purely decorative pattern.

### Daylight without excessive glare

Natural daylight is valuable, but uncontrolled sunlight can create glare and additional cooling loads. Window placement, shading and internal layouts should be coordinated to create visually comfortable working conditions.

### Appropriate ventilation and humidity control

Natural ventilation may be useful when outdoor conditions are suitable. In other situations, filtered mechanical ventilation and dehumidification may be necessary. These decisions should be coordinated with the building-services design.

### Integrating vegetation and outdoor spaces

Courtyards, green terraces, shaded walkways and accessible gardens can improve visual connections with nature and provide additional spaces for employees to rest and interact.

### Post-occupancy evaluation

A building's performance should not be judged only by its drawings or design specifications. After occupation, actual indoor conditions and user experiences should be evaluated. Temperature, humidity, air movement, lighting, indoor air quality, acoustic comfort and occupant satisfaction can all provide useful information for future design decisions.

## 8. The Role of Building Standards and Environmental Assessment

Bangladesh has already taken steps towards improving building energy performance.

The **Bangladesh National Building Code (BNBC) 2020** includes provisions relating to energy efficiency, building-envelope performance, building equipment and environmental considerations.

The **Sustainable and Renewable Energy Development Authority (SREDA)** has also developed the *Building Energy Efficiency and Environment Rating (BEEER)* guideline for new and existing buildings. Its Version II guideline was published in January 2023.

These frameworks provide useful references for architects and developers working towards more sustainable buildings.

However, meeting a design requirement and achieving a comfortable indoor environment are not necessarily the same thing.

Buildings need to be evaluated during operation. Environmental measurements, energy monitoring and feedback from occupants can reveal whether the original design decisions are working as intended.

This is where post-occupancy evaluation becomes particularly important.

It allows architects to learn from completed projects rather than repeating the same assumptions in every new development.

## Conclusion: Designing Workplaces That Respond to People and Climate

Air conditioning will remain an important part of commercial architecture in Bangladesh. The objective should not be to eliminate mechanical cooling, but to reduce the unnecessary demand created by inappropriate building design.

A more effective approach begins with understanding the site, the local climate and the people who will occupy the building.

Facade orientation, solar shading, daylight, ventilation, material selection, courtyards and vegetation should be considered together. Mechanical systems should then support the building's environmental strategy rather than compensate for every limitation in its design.

My [research on the Rangpur Hi-Tech Park](/research) helped me recognise that the quality of a workplace cannot be measured simply by its floor area, facilities or cooling capacity. It also depends on how people experience the spaces in which they spend their working lives.

As Bangladesh continues to develop its commercial and technology infrastructure, architects have an opportunity to rethink the conventional office model.

The next generation of workplaces should not simply be colder. They should be healthier, more environmentally responsive and more closely connected to the people who use them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### 1. Why do office buildings in Bangladesh need climate-responsive design?

Bangladesh experiences high temperatures, humidity and seasonal climatic variations. Climate-responsive design can help reduce unwanted solar heat gain, manage daylight and support appropriate ventilation, thereby reducing unnecessary dependence on mechanical cooling.

### 2. Can passive cooling replace air conditioning in Bangladesh?

Not in every building or throughout every season. Passive strategies such as shading, building orientation, courtyards and natural ventilation can reduce cooling demand, but many commercial buildings will still require mechanical cooling and humidity control.

### 3. What is the ideal window-to-wall ratio for office buildings in Bangladesh?

A study by Rana et al. (2022) suggested a 30–40% window-to-wall ratio for the air-conditioned office prototype it examined. The appropriate ratio for an individual building depends on orientation, glazing, shading and environmental requirements.

### 4. How does biophilic design improve workplace environments?

Biophilic design integrates natural elements and experiences into buildings. These may include vegetation, daylight, natural views, water and connections to outdoor spaces. Such features can support a more restorative and engaging workplace environment.

### 5. What is the importance of indoor air quality in office buildings?

Indoor air quality is influenced by ventilation, filtration, pollutant sources, humidity and occupancy. Appropriate ventilation helps remove indoor contaminants and maintain healthier conditions for building occupants.
