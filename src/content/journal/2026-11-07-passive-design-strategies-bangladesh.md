An office can have air conditioning running all day and still feel uncomfortable. The room may be cool, but the light is harsh. The windows may be large, but the desks are too far from them to offer a useful view. The air may feel still, and there may be nowhere to step away from a screen for a few minutes.

These problems are often treated as issues to solve after construction. A tenant adds blinds, buys better chairs or installs another air-conditioning unit. Some improvements help. Others are difficult or expensive because the building itself was never planned around the people using it.

In Bangladesh, where heat, humidity and seasonal rain shape everyday life, the design of an office deserves more attention from the beginning. The position of the building, the depth of its floor plates, the way it handles sunlight and the space it gives to trees and outdoor air all influence how it feels to work there.

This is where passive design strategies in Bangladesh can make a practical difference. They use the building's form, openings, shading and landscape to reduce unwanted heat and make better use of daylight and natural air. As discussed in [Why Office Buildings in Bangladesh Need More Than Air Conditioning](/blogs/climate-responsive-office-design-bangladesh), they will not remove the need for mechanical cooling in every season or every building. They can, however, reduce the burden placed on those systems and make workplaces more pleasant to use.

## Why workplace design needs a closer look

The conversation about office design often begins with floor area, rent, furniture and the number of people a building can accommodate. Those things matter, but they do not describe the full experience of working inside it.

During my [research for a proposed human-centred workplace at Rangpur Hi-Tech Park](/research), I surveyed 30 IT and IT-enabled services professionals from nine technology firms. The survey asked about their existing work environments, common discomforts and the improvements they valued most.

Light, ventilation and greenery received the lowest average rating among the existing workplace dimensions, at **2.93 out of 5**. When respondents had to choose the single most important improvement, **26.7%** selected better ventilation and temperature control. Another **26.7%** selected views of greenery from their workstations.

The survey also recorded self-reported symptoms during a typical workweek. Back or neck discomfort was reported by **86.7%** of respondents, eye strain after screen work by **80%**, and work-related stress or mental fatigue by **76.7%** at least sometimes.

The figures describe the experiences of these 30 professionals. They should not be read as a picture of every office worker in Bangladesh, and the symptoms are self-reported rather than medical diagnoses. Even with those limits, the survey points to issues worth considering in the design brief: daylight, air movement and access to nature (for a closer look at the survey findings, see [Can Workplace Design Influence Employee Wellbeing? Lessons from Rangpur](/blogs/workplace-wellbeing-architecture-bangladesh-rangpur)).

## Start with the climate, not the façade style

Bangladesh does not have one steady climate throughout the year. Hot pre-monsoon months bring strong sun and high temperatures. The monsoon brings heavy rain and persistent humidity. Winter is cooler, especially in the north, where cold winds can make open spaces uncomfortable.

A building that works well in one season may need a different response in another. Opening every window is not a complete ventilation strategy. Large areas of glass do not guarantee useful daylight. A courtyard can become an uncomfortable heat trap if it is poorly proportioned or left without shade.

Good climate-responsive architecture begins by understanding the site: where the sun reaches the building, which way the seasonal winds move, how rainwater travels across the land, what trees already exist and how nearby buildings affect air and light.

The following five strategies are a useful starting point for offices and other shared workplaces in Bangladesh.

### 1. Place the building carefully and avoid excessively deep plans

The first decisions are often made before the façade is designed. A building's orientation and overall shape determine how much sun its walls receive and how far daylight and fresh air can travel inside.

For many sites in Bangladesh, an east-west building axis is a useful starting point. It can leave the longer façades facing north and south while reducing the amount of wall exposed to low-angle morning and afternoon sun on the east and west. The exact arrangement still needs to be checked against the site's surroundings, local sun path, wind direction and neighbouring buildings.

The depth of the floor plan matters just as much. In a very deep office, people working near the centre may sit far from windows. Daylight becomes harder to share across the floor, and air has a longer route to travel. The result can be a workplace that depends heavily on artificial lighting and mechanical ventilation.

Breaking a large building into narrower wings can bring more rooms closer to an external wall or courtyard. It also creates opportunities for cross-ventilation and views outside. This approach may use more perimeter wall and circulation area, so the design needs to balance environmental quality with construction cost and usable floor space.

The useful question is simple: can people in the middle of the building see daylight, find a view and benefit from air movement where conditions allow?

### 2. Give fresh air a clear route through the building

When air is still, even a room at an acceptable temperature can feel uncomfortable. In warm and humid conditions, moving air across the body can improve comfort by helping sweat evaporate.

Cross-ventilation works when air can enter from one side and leave through another. Windows on opposite or adjacent sides, open corridors and carefully placed courtyards can help create that route. The building's layout must respond to the prevailing seasonal winds. A window alone cannot guarantee ventilation if another wall, partition or building blocks the path.

Offices also need to manage rain, noise, privacy, insects and outdoor air quality. A permanently open office may not be suitable beside a busy road or during heavy rain. Quiet rooms need protection from conversations and activity in shared areas. These details should be considered alongside the movement of air.

A mixed-mode approach is often more realistic for Bangladesh. Natural ventilation and ceiling fans can be used when outdoor conditions are suitable. Mechanical cooling can support the building during very hot and humid periods or when outdoor air is unsuitable. The aim is to give the building more than one way to stay comfortable.

### 3. Stop harsh sunlight before it reaches the glass

Windows bring daylight and views into an office. They can also bring unwanted heat and glare, particularly when they are large and exposed to direct sun.

Internal blinds can reduce glare, but they do not prevent sunlight from passing through the glass and heating the space first. External shading is often more effective because it blocks or filters sunlight before it reaches the window.

The shading should respond to the direction of the façade. Horizontal overhangs can help control higher-angle sun. Vertical fins, screens and deeper shaded edges can be more useful against low-angle sunlight from the east and west. The right combination depends on the building's orientation, window size and surrounding context.

Bangladesh's traditional verandah offers a familiar lesson. A deep roofed edge can shade the wall, protect openings from rain and create a place to sit outside the main interior. It also gives people a gradual transition between indoors and outdoors.

A verandah does not need to be copied as a nostalgic feature. Its depth, materials and position can be designed for a contemporary office. What matters is that the building provides shade where people and windows need it.

### 4. Make the courtyard part of everyday work life

The *uthon*, or courtyard, has long been part of homes and settlements across Bengal. It gives daylight and air access to the spaces around it. It is also a shared place where people meet, pause and carry out everyday activities.

A courtyard can play a similar role in a workplace. It can bring light into the centre of a building, create views from work areas and provide a sheltered outdoor place for a short break. When surrounded by narrower building wings, it can also help connect rooms to air and landscape.

Its success depends on the way it is designed. A courtyard that is too small, exposed or difficult to reach may become little more than a decorative gap. Shade, planting, seating, drainage and access from the workplace all matter. The courtyard should feel like a useful shared room without a roof.

In my [proposed Rangpur Hi-Tech Park design](/work), the main building functions were arranged in separate blocks around a central courtyard. The idea was to bring more workspaces close to daylight and views while giving employees a shared outdoor space. It was an academic design proposal, not a completed or evaluated building, but it helped me explore how the courtyard could shape the whole plan rather than being added at the end.

### 5. Keep existing trees and make landscape part of the design

Trees are often treated as decoration to be added after the building footprint is fixed. On a hot site, they should be considered much earlier.

A mature tree can shade a walkway, reduce the amount of sun falling on paving and make an outdoor space more comfortable. Planting can also soften the edge between a building and its surroundings. Green roofs and planted terraces may add useful outdoor space, although they need suitable structural design, waterproofing and long-term maintenance.

The first step is to understand what is already growing on the site. Existing trees take years to replace. Retaining healthy vegetation can be more sensible than clearing a plot and trying to recreate the same landscape later.

In the [Rangpur Hi-Tech Park study](/research), the site analysis recorded existing fruit trees, including jackfruit, mango, lemon and banana. The proposed plan retained mature trees along parts of the boundary and included a courtyard pond to hold rainwater. The intention was to work with the site's existing character and make better use of monsoon rain. Any real project would still need detailed checks for drainage, water quality, mosquito control, planting suitability and maintenance.

Landscape works best when it is connected to the daily life of the building. A shaded path that employees actually use may be more valuable than a large planted area that is difficult to reach.

## What these strategies look like when they work together

Orientation, ventilation, shading, courtyards and planting are often discussed as separate design features. In practice, they depend on one another.

A narrow floor plate can bring work areas closer to windows. External shading can make those windows more useful by reducing glare. A courtyard can provide a view and create a route for air, while trees make the space more comfortable during breaks. The result is a building where environmental decisions support the way people use the place.

This was the direction I explored in my [Rangpur Hi-Tech Park proposal](/work). The design divided the programme into four blocks around a central courtyard, with shaded circulation, planted areas and shared spaces. The proposal also recognised that natural ventilation would not be suitable all the time. During the hottest and most humid periods, the office floors could still need mechanical cooling.

That qualification matters. Passive design is not a guarantee that a building can operate without air conditioning. It is a way to reduce avoidable heat, use daylight more carefully and make natural conditions available when they are comfortable and safe.

## A better design brief begins before construction

Many workplace problems are difficult to fix after the structure is complete. A tenant can replace a chair or add a task light. It is much harder to change the building's orientation, create a courtyard or bring a window closer to a workstation once the floor plan has been built.

For clients and developers, this means asking better questions at the briefing stage. Where will the building receive the strongest sun? Can the floor plate be shallower? Will windows be protected from rain? Can employees reach a shaded outdoor space? Which existing trees can be retained? How will the building work during the monsoon as well as the dry season?

Architects should test these questions through site analysis, sun studies, ventilation planning and coordination with structural and building-services engineers. The details need to be adapted to each site and checked against the relevant building regulations. A strategy that works in Rangpur may need adjustment in Dhaka or Chattogram.

For Bangladesh's growing workplaces, climate-responsive design is also a question of everyday experience. Employees spend a large part of their day inside these buildings. The places where they work should support concentration, comfort and a healthy connection with the outdoors.

For an office in Bangladesh, the first comfort decisions are made at the drawing board: where to place the building, how to shade it, where air can move and which parts of the site should remain green.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is passive design in Bangladesh?

Passive design uses a building's orientation, shape, openings, shading and landscape to respond to local climate. In Bangladesh, common considerations include strong sun, high humidity, seasonal monsoon rain and changing wind directions.

### Can an office in Bangladesh work without air conditioning?

Some offices may use natural ventilation and fans for parts of the year. During very hot and humid periods, or when outdoor air is polluted or noisy, mechanical cooling may still be needed. The building should be designed to use each approach when conditions are suitable.

### Which passive design strategy should be considered first?

Start with the site, orientation and building form. These decisions influence daylight, solar exposure and ventilation, and they are difficult to change after construction. The best solution depends on the site's sun, wind, surroundings and intended use.

### Why are courtyards useful in office design?

A well-designed courtyard can bring daylight and views into surrounding spaces. It can also provide a shared outdoor area for short breaks and informal meetings. Its size, shade, planting, drainage and access determine whether people will use it.

### Does planting make an office building cooler?

Trees and planting can shade outdoor surfaces and reduce heat absorbed by paving and walls. Green roofs and planted terraces may also help, but they require suitable detailing and maintenance. Planting should be planned as part of the building and site design.
