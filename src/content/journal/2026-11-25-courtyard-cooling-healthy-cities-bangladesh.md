## Can a Courtyard Cool a Bangladeshi Home by 2 Degrees?

The fan was running. The room was still hot.

I have felt that in many Bangladeshi homes and offices. We add a fan, then an air conditioner, then a bigger air conditioner. The bill grows. The heat stays.

In Bogura, a client once asked me to remove the courtyard from his house plan. He wanted more floor area instead. That courtyard was the only open space where air could move and light could enter. I kept thinking about that choice. It was not only about one house. It was about how we build our cities.

New research from 2025 gives that question a clear answer. Design can cool a home, calm the mind, and make a city healthier, without depending only on machines.

## Buildings Change How We Feel

A study by Xing and colleagues, published in PLOS ONE on 4 March 2025, looked at biophilic building design. Biophilic design means bringing nature into buildings, through plants, daylight, natural materials, views of greenery and open air.

The study found that biophilic designs promote wellbeing and stimulate inspiration. Designs without biophilic features had adverse psychological effects. In simple words, a room with plants, light and air helps us feel better. A closed concrete box does the opposite.

A second review, the Humanise Global Evidence Review from September 2025, looked at more than 80 studies. It found that building exteriors and facades affect stress, attention, memory, mood and physiology. The outside of a building is not only decoration. It changes how people feel inside it and on the street outside it.

A study from Jinan City, reported in 2026, studied 512 residents in high density housing. It found that biophilic neighbourhood design enhanced psychological wellbeing. Natural preference had the largest direct effect. People did not only want a green room. They wanted a green neighbourhood.

## The Courtyard Is Not Old Fashioned. It Is Passive Cooling.

My thesis work in Rangpur gave me a local reason to trust this. I surveyed 30 employees from 9 companies during the 2020 lockdown. They reported eye strain in 80 percent of cases, back and neck pain in 86.7 percent, and stress in 76.7 percent. Light, ventilation and greenery scored lowest in their workplaces.

From that survey I derived 18 design attributes, and I designed a courtyard scheme for 200 employees at Khalishakuri, Rangpur, on an 8.59 acre site. The courtyard was not a decoration. It was the centre of light, air and rest.

New research now supports that idea with numbers.

Sun and colleagues, in the Journal of Building Engineering in 2025, studied courtyard roof shapes. A dome roof gave 80 percent higher indoor wind speed than a flat roof. It lowered the average indoor temperature by up to 2.1 degrees Celsius. A parapet roof was worst, with an average of 26.4 degrees Celsius and the lowest wind speed.

That is a large difference from shape alone. No extra electricity. No extra machine. Only the form of the roof.

Another study, by Aloshan and Aldali in Scientific Reports in 2025, ran nearly 29,000 parametric simulations across 5 hot and warm climates. It found that window to wall ratio and insulation accounted for over 40 percent of the variance in performance. Courtyard dimensions alone accounted for under 3 percent.

That tells us something practical. A courtyard helps, but only if the rest of the design is right. The size of windows, the shading, and the insulation matter more than making the courtyard a little bigger or smaller.

## What This Means for Dhaka

Dhaka is hot, dense and often flooded. Experts in Dhaka now urge rooftop and vertical gardening to reduce the urban heat island effect. The RAJUK Climate Resilient Green Action Plan and the Detailed Area Plan 2022 to 2035 point in the same direction. Canals are described as the veins of Dhaka. When they are filled or blocked, the city cannot breathe or drain.

A dighi, a pond, is not only a water body. It cools the air around it, holds rainwater, and gives people a place to sit, walk and meet. A rooftop garden cools the roof below it. A shaded street makes walking and cycling possible, even in summer.

These are not luxury ideas. They are low cost, local and proven in our climate.

## Five Practical Steps

1. Keep the courtyard. In a house, keep an open uthan, even a small one. In an office, keep an open court or a shaded atrium. Let air move through it.
2. Choose the roof with care. A dome or a well ventilated roof can move more air than a flat roof. Avoid a high parapet that traps hot air.
3. Shade the windows first. Use deep eaves, bamboo screens, jaali and planting. Shading is cheaper than cooling a room after the sun has heated it.
4. Bring plants to every level. A tree in the courtyard, plants on the balcony, and a garden on the roof all help. Choose native plants that survive heat and rain.
5. Protect water. Keep a dighi, a pond or a rain garden where possible. Do not fill a canal or a pond for one more room. That room will be hotter without it.

## A Healthy City Starts at Home

A healthy city is not built only by large projects. It starts with one courtyard, one shaded window, one rooftop garden, one pond that is not filled in.

My work now moves from buildings to planning. I want to study Urban Planning and Policy Design, and Sustainable Architecture and Landscape Design, so that these small choices become city policy, not only personal choice.

The fan in that hot room taught me a simple lesson. We cannot cool Bangladesh with machines alone. We need to design with air, light, shade, water and greenery, as our courtyards always did.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Can a courtyard really cool a house in Bangladesh?
Yes, if it is designed well. Research in 2025 found a dome roof courtyard gave 80 percent higher indoor wind speed than a flat roof and lowered indoor temperature by up to 2.1 degrees Celsius.

Is biophilic design only about plants?
No. It includes daylight, natural ventilation, natural materials, views of nature, and open space. Plants are one part of it.

What matters most in a courtyard house?
Window size and shading, and insulation, matter most. In one study of nearly 29,000 simulations, those factors explained over 40 percent of performance. Courtyard size alone explained under 3 percent.

Can rooftop gardens help Dhaka?
Yes. Experts urge rooftop and vertical gardening to reduce the urban heat island effect in Dhaka. A green roof also cools the rooms below it and can hold rainwater.

What did the Rangpur survey find?
In my thesis survey of 30 employees, 80 percent reported eye strain, 86.7 percent reported back and neck pain, and 76.7 percent reported stress. Light, ventilation and greenery scored lowest.
