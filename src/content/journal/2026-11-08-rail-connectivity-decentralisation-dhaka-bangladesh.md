Anyone who has travelled between northern Bangladesh and Dhaka knows how much of a day the journey can take. A visit for a meeting, a university appointment or a job interview can involve an overnight train, a long road trip or careful planning around the return journey. For people living in Dinajpur or Rangpur, distance is not simply measured in kilometres. It is measured in time, lost workdays and the opportunities that become harder to reach.

That is why the proposed Bogura–Sirajganj railway connection matters. It is intended to shorten the indirect route used by trains travelling between the north-west and Dhaka. But the bigger question is more interesting: could better rail connectivity help people build their lives and careers outside the capital?

I began thinking about this while working on my architectural thesis for the proposed Rangpur Hi-Tech Park. The project made me consider how transport affects the design of a workplace, where employees can live and whether a regional city can attract companies that would otherwise choose Dhaka.

A faster train alone will not decentralise Bangladesh. It could, however, become part of a wider plan that connects regional jobs, affordable travel and better-designed workplaces.

## What is changing on the Dhaka–north route?

The Dhaka–Dinajpur journey still takes a substantial part of the day. Current timetable listings show direct services taking roughly seven and a half to almost nine hours, depending on the train. Timetables can change, so passengers should always check the latest Bangladesh Railway information before travelling. [Current route listings](https://railmatebd.com/en/train/dhka-to-dnj) and [timetable information](https://ghuredekha.com/en/train/dhaka-dinajpur) provide a useful snapshot of the journey.

The proposed Bogura–Sirajganj line is intended to reduce the long detour. According to the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB), the plan is for an approximately 86-kilometre dual-gauge connection with 11 stations. The AIIB project page currently lists it as proposed, and describes the goal as improving connectivity and reducing journey time between the northern districts and Dhaka. It does not promise a four-hour Dhaka–Dinajpur timetable. [AIIB project information](https://www.aiib.org/en/projects/details/2026/proposed/bangladesh-construction-of-dual-gauge-railway-line-from-bogura-to-sirajganj-project.html).

A Bangladesh Public Procurement Authority notice also lists a consultancy assignment for the project, expected to start in the second quarter of 2027. This is a sign that important preparation and delivery work remains ahead. The line should therefore be discussed as a planned connection, not as a railway that is about to deliver a confirmed four-hour journey. [Procurement notice](https://bppa.gov.bd/advertisement-services/details-10694.html).

The route has real potential. The final travel time will depend on the completed alignment, the condition and capacity of connecting lines, train speeds, station stops and the timetable Bangladesh Railway can operate reliably.

## What Rangpur taught me about the cost of distance

In my Bachelor of Architecture thesis, *Designing a Human-Centered Work Environment for the Employees of Rangpur Hi-Tech Park*, I studied how a technology workplace might support the growth of Rangpur as a regional employment centre.

One practical problem appeared early in the site and context analysis: travel between Dhaka and the north made short visits difficult. A company considering a regional office might need to bring managers, trainers or specialists from the capital. If the journey takes most of a day, even a meeting or a short training session becomes expensive in time.

In my academic design proposal, I included a dormitory block with 27 living units for visiting staff and trainees. That was a design response to the transport conditions I was studying. It was not part of an official built project, and it was not evidence that a dormitory would solve the wider connectivity problem.

The exercise left me with a broader planning question. If regional cities are expected to attract employers, they need more than office buildings. They need dependable links to the rest of the country, good local transport, housing, education, healthcare and places where skilled people can build a long-term life.

## Faster rail could make hybrid work more practical

A two- or three-hour journey is sometimes described as the point at which daily commuting becomes possible. I would treat that as a planning ambition, not a fixed rule. A two-hour train trip each way is still four hours of travel before someone has reached the office, and that may not be realistic every day.

The more immediate opportunity may be hybrid commuting. A professional could work most of the week from a regional office or home, then travel to Dhaka for an important meeting, training session or a day with a central team. Companies could also keep smaller offices in the capital while building stronger teams in places such as Rangpur, Dinajpur, Rajshahi and Bogura.

For this to work, services need to be frequent and dependable. A journey that is fast on paper but regularly delayed will not give employers or employees the confidence to plan around it. Departure times should connect with working hours, and the return service should make a same-day visit possible when the distance allows.

## The missing piece: affordable commuter passes

Travel time matters, but the cost of repeated travel matters just as much. Consider a person who needs to make 22 return journeys in a month. That means 44 one-way trips. Buying a separate ticket for every journey could become expensive, especially for someone travelling in a more comfortable seat or paying for last-minute availability.

A monthly commuter pass could make rail more useful for people who travel regularly. Bangladesh Railway could study a few options:

- **A hybrid pass** with a fixed number of return journeys each month for people travelling once or twice a week.
- **A corridor pass** for frequent commuters, with a clear route, seat-allocation rules and limits that match available capacity.
- **An employer-supported pass** where a company pays part of the travel cost as an employee transport benefit.

A BDT 5,000–10,000 monthly price range appeared in my original proposal as a possible starting point for discussion. It should be treated as a suggested target for a feasibility study, not an approved Bangladesh Railway fare or a costed financial plan. The actual price would need to account for operating costs, demand, seat availability, subsidies and the effect on ordinary passengers.

A flexible pass may be a more sensible first step than unlimited travel. It could support hybrid workers without promising more seats than the railway can provide. Any employer contribution should also be developed through local policy and consultation; it is a proposal, not an existing legal entitlement in Bangladesh.

## Design the train for the journey people actually make

If people are expected to spend several hours on a train, comfort should be part of the service design. I call this idea a “rolling office”, although a train does not need to become a full office to make the journey more useful.

A practical commuter coach could include supportive seats, a small fold-down table, charging points, reliable mobile connectivity, good reading light and places for bags. These basics would help passengers read, answer messages or work for short periods without having to balance a laptop on their knees.

Quiet areas could be useful for passengers who want to rest or concentrate. If space allows, a separate area for phone calls would prevent conversations from disturbing everyone else. Good ventilation, clean toilets, safe luggage storage and clear passenger information are just as important as Wi-Fi.

There are limits. Train movement, noise and shared seating may make detailed computer work uncomfortable. Not everyone wants to work while travelling. A better goal is to give passengers a comfortable choice: work a little, read, rest or look out of the window.

## Regional cities need to be ready for the opportunity

Improved rail links could bring more visitors, students, workers and businesses to northern Bangladesh. But if people arrive at a station and find no reliable way to reach an office, campus or home, much of the benefit is lost.

Stations should connect with local buses, auto-rickshaws, rickshaws, walking routes and safe cycle parking. Shaded pedestrian access, clear wayfinding and well-organised pick-up areas can make the last part of a journey easier. Regional authorities and employers should also consider shuttle links to major employment areas where demand justifies them.

The cities themselves need investment. Better broadband, universities, hospitals, schools, public spaces and affordable housing all help people choose to stay. Regional development should create good reasons to live and work outside Dhaka, rather than assuming that a faster train will make those places attractive on its own.

For architects, this is where transport planning and building design meet. Regional workplaces should respond to local climate, provide useful shared spaces and connect to public transport. We should avoid reproducing the same congested, car-dependent office districts that already create problems in the capital.

## A more connected Bangladesh needs more than a railway line

The proposed Bogura–Sirajganj connection is an important opportunity to improve the north's connection with the national rail network. But a four-hour Dhaka–Dinajpur journey is not a confirmed outcome, and a faster service alone will not solve the concentration of jobs and services in the capital.

The wider idea is worth pursuing: reliable rail services, affordable passes for regular travellers, employer support, comfortable commuter coaches and regional cities with the services and jobs people need. These elements must be planned together.

As an architect, my interest is in what happens after the train arrives. Can someone build a career in Rangpur? Can a company operate there without depending on daily visits from Dhaka? Can a young professional stay close to family while still accessing national opportunities?

If better rail links help make those choices realistic, they will do more than shorten a journey. They will give Bangladesh more than one centre of opportunity.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will the Bogura–Sirajganj line reduce the Dhaka–Dinajpur journey to four hours?

The line is intended to shorten the indirect rail route, but an official four-hour Dhaka–Dinajpur timetable has not been confirmed in the project information reviewed for this article. The final journey time will depend on the completed line and the service Bangladesh Railway operates.

### What is a monthly commuter pass?

It is a ticket subscription that lets a passenger make a set number of journeys, or travel on an agreed route, during a defined period. A hybrid pass could be designed for people who travel to Dhaka only once or twice a week.

### Could faster trains help decentralise Dhaka?

They could support decentralisation if they are linked to regional jobs, reliable local transport, good public services and affordable housing. Faster journeys alone will not move companies or employment opportunities out of the capital.

### What is a “rolling office” train?

It is a commuter train designed to make longer journeys more comfortable and useful. Features could include supportive seats, fold-down tables, power outlets, reliable connectivity, good lighting and separate quiet and call areas where space allows.
